Traditional AI Techniques

The classical toolkit of artificial intelligence: search algorithms, formal logic, probabilistic reasoning, and statistical classification — the methods that preceded deep learning and remain essential today.

Before neural networks, there were algorithms.

AI researchers have developed a rich collection of algorithmic techniques for solving problems associated with intelligence. These traditional methods — search, logic, probability, and classification — remain fundamental to AI even as deep learning has become dominant. Understanding them is essential for understanding the full scope of the field.

01 — Search & Optimisation

Many AI problems are search problems.

At its core, much of AI is about searching through a space of possible solutions to find one that satisfies some criteria. Whether planning a route, solving a puzzle, or optimising a function, search algorithms are the workhorse of traditional AI.

Search
The process of systematically exploring a space of possible states or solutions to find one that meets a goal condition or optimises an objective function.
The combinatorial explosion

Most interesting AI problems have exponentially many possible solutions. A chess game has roughly 10120 possible positions — more than atoms in the observable universe. Search algorithms must find good solutions without examining every possibility. This is the fundamental challenge of AI.

02 — State Space Search

Exploring a tree of possibilities.

State space search searches through a tree of possible states to try to find a goal state. Planning algorithms search through trees of goals and subgoals, attempting to find a path to a target goal.

Figure 03 · State space search explores a tree of possible states to find a path to the goal

Breadth-first search

Explores all states at the current depth before moving deeper. Guarantees finding the shortest path but requires exponential memory. Good when the solution is shallow.

Depth-first search

Explores one branch fully before backtracking. Uses less memory but may miss shorter paths or get stuck in infinite branches. Good when solutions are deep.

A* search

Uses a heuristic function to estimate the cost to reach the goal from each state. Explores the most promising states first. Widely used in pathfinding, game AI, and robotics. Optimal when the heuristic is admissible (never overestimates).

Adversarial search

For games and competitive situations. Minimax algorithms search through possible moves, assuming the opponent plays optimally. Alpha-beta pruning makes this tractable by eliminating branches that cannot affect the final decision.

03 — Local Search

Optimising by moving toward better solutions.

Local search starts with an initial solution and iteratively improves it by making small changes. Rather than exploring all possibilities, it moves through the solution space toward a local optimum.

Gradient Descent
An optimisation algorithm that iteratively adjusts parameters in the direction that most rapidly decreases a loss function. It is the foundation of neural network training and many other machine learning methods.
Figure 04 · Gradient descent follows the slope of the loss function — but may converge to a local minimum rather than the global one

Hill climbing

Always move to the best neighbouring solution. Simple but gets stuck in local optima — a solution that is better than its neighbours but not the best overall.

Simulated annealing

Occasionally accepts worse solutions to escape local optima, inspired by the annealing process in metallurgy. The probability of accepting worse solutions decreases over time.

Evolutionary algorithms

Maintain a population of solutions, combining and mutating them over generations. Inspired by biological evolution. Effective for complex optimisation problems with many local optima.

04 — Formal Logic

Reasoning with precise rules.

Formal logic is used for reasoning and knowledge representation. It comes in two main forms: propositional logic (which operates on statements that are true or false and uses logical connectives) and predicate logic (which also operates on objects, relations, and quantifiers).

Propositional Logic
A formal system where statements (propositions) are either true or false, combined using operators like AND, OR, NOT, and IMPLIES. Example: "If it rains AND the roof leaks, THEN the floor is wet."

Propositional logic

Operates on simple true/false statements with logical connectives. Useful for rule-based systems, verification, and simple reasoning tasks. Limited because it cannot represent objects, quantities, or relationships between entities.

Example: IF temperature > 100 AND pressure > normal THEN alert(operator)

Predicate logic (first-order)

Extends propositional logic with variables, objects, relations, and quantifiers (∀ "for all", ∃ "there exists"). Can express complex knowledge about the world.

Example: ∀x (Human(x) → Mortal(x)); Human(Socrates); ∴ Mortal(Socrates)

Fuzzy logic

Assigns a degree of truth between 0 and 1, rather than strict true/false. Useful for systems that must handle vague or imprecise concepts like "warm," "fast," or "tall." Widely used in control systems (washing machines, air conditioning, automotive systems).

Limitations of logic

Logic requires explicit knowledge encoding — someone must write all the rules. It struggles with uncertainty, ambiguity, and the frame problem (determining what is relevant). These limitations drove the shift toward statistical and learning-based approaches.

05 — Probabilistic Methods

Reasoning under uncertainty.

Many problems in AI (including reasoning, planning, learning, perception, and robotics) require the agent to operate with incomplete or uncertain information. Probabilistic methods provide mathematical tools for this.

Bayesian Network
A directed acyclic graph where nodes represent random variables and edges represent probabilistic dependencies. It compactly encodes joint probability distributions and supports efficient inference.
Figure 05 · A Bayesian network: given that the grass is wet, what is the probability it rained vs. the sprinkler was on?

Bayes' theorem

Updates beliefs based on evidence: P(H|E) = P(E|H) × P(H) / P(E). The foundation of probabilistic AI — it tells us how to revise our estimates when we observe new data.

Markov models

Model sequences where the next state depends only on the current state (Markov property). Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) are widely used in speech recognition and natural language processing.

Decision theory

Combines probabilities with utilities (values) to make optimal decisions under uncertainty. Expected utility = Σ P(outcome) × Utility(outcome). The rational agent chooses the action with highest expected utility.

06 — Classifiers

Categorising inputs into classes.

The simplest AI applications can be divided into two types: classifiers (e.g., "if shiny then diamond") and controllers (e.g., "if diamond then pick up"). Classifiers are functions that use pattern matching to determine the closest match for a given input.

Decision trees

A tree of yes/no questions that leads to a classification. Interpretable and fast, but can overfit training data. Random forests combine many trees for better accuracy.

Support vector machines

Find the hyperplane that best separates classes in feature space. Effective for high-dimensional data. Were state-of-the-art for many tasks before deep learning.

k-Nearest neighbours

Classify an input by finding the k most similar examples in the training data and taking a vote. Simple, intuitive, but computationally expensive for large datasets.

The bridge to learning

Classifiers can be hand-designed with rules or trained from data using statistical learning methods. The shift from hand-designed to data-trained classifiers was one of the key transitions in AI history. → Note 04: Machine Learning

07 — Infrastructure

Hardware and software for AI.

In the late 2010s, graphics processing units (GPUs) that were increasingly designed with tensor operations started being used to accelerate neural networks, overtaking CPUs as the dominant platform for AI workloads.

Hardware evolution

AI hardware has evolved from general-purpose CPUs to specialised accelerators. GPUs provide massive parallelism for matrix operations. TPUs are custom-designed for neural network training. Specialised chips (Groq, Cerebras) target specific AI workloads with even greater efficiency.

Software ecosystem

Key languages include Python (dominant), C++ (performance), and specialised languages like Prolog (logic programming) and Lisp (symbolic AI). Frameworks like PyTorch, TensorFlow, and scikit-learn provide the building blocks for modern AI development.

The hardware bottleneck

Modern AI is constrained by hardware availability. Training a frontier model requires thousands of GPUs running for months. This creates a significant barrier to entry and concentrates AI development in well-funded organisations. → Note 05: Modern AI Systems

08 — Comparison

Symbolic vs. statistical approaches.

AI has historically been divided into two broad paradigms: symbolic (rule-based, logical) and statistical (data-driven, probabilistic). Understanding their complementary strengths is essential.

AspectSymbolic AIStatistical / Learning AI
Knowledge sourceHand-encoded rules and logicLearned from data
ReasoningDeductive, precise, provableInductive, approximate, probabilistic
TransparencyHigh — rules are human-readableLow — learned representations are opaque
Uncertainty handlingDifficult (requires extensions like fuzzy logic)Natural (built into probabilistic frameworks)
Perception tasksPoor — hard to encode visual/auditory patternsExcellent — deep learning excels here
Common-sense reasoningRequires encoding vast amounts of knowledgeLearns implicit knowledge from data
BrittlenessFails when rules don't cover the caseGraceful degradation, but can hallucinate
Data requirementsLow (needs expert knowledge instead)High (needs large training datasets)
ScalabilityExpensive to maintain as rules growScales with data and compute
Best suited forFormal reasoning, verification, planningPerception, language, pattern recognition
The modern synthesis

Modern AI systems increasingly combine both paradigms. Large language models (statistical) can be augmented with symbolic reasoning tools, code interpreters, and structured knowledge bases. The either/or debate is giving way to hybrid approaches that leverage the strengths of each. → Note 09: Philosophy

Remember these ideas.

01

Many AI problems are search problems — finding a path through a space of possible solutions. Search algorithms must handle combinatorial explosion.

02

State space search explores a tree of states (A*, minimax); local search iteratively improves a solution (gradient descent, simulated annealing).

03

Formal logic provides precise reasoning but requires explicit knowledge encoding and struggles with uncertainty and ambiguity.

04

Probabilistic methods (Bayesian networks, decision theory) enable reasoning under uncertainty — essential for real-world AI systems.

05

Symbolic and statistical approaches have complementary strengths. Modern AI increasingly combines both rather than choosing one paradigm.

06

Hardware constraints (GPUs, compute costs) shape what AI is possible — and who can build it.
