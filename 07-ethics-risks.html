Ethics

Ethics, Risks & Harms

What can go wrong with artificial intelligence — from immediate harms like bias and misinformation to long-term concerns about unemployment, environmental impact, and existential risk.

Capability does not mean reliability.

AI systems can be remarkably useful while still failing in systematic ways. As AI becomes more powerful and pervasive, understanding its risks is not optional — it is essential for anyone who builds, deploys, regulates, or uses these systems. This note examines the full spectrum of AI risks, from immediate harms to existential concerns.

01 — Privacy & Copyright

Data hunger creates privacy risks.

Machine learning algorithms require large amounts of data. The techniques used to acquire this data have raised concerns about privacy and copyright. AI systems are trained on vast datasets scraped from the internet, including personal information, copyrighted works, and private communications.

Data Privacy
The risk that AI training data includes personal information collected without consent, and that models may memorize and reproduce sensitive data in their outputs.

Privacy concerns

AI systems can infer private information from seemingly innocuous data. Facial recognition enables mass surveillance. Language models can memorize and regurgitate training data, including personal details. Data brokers feed AI systems with information people never consented to share.

Copyright disputes

Major lawsuits have been filed by authors, artists, publishers, and media companies against AI companies for training models on copyrighted works without permission or compensation. The legal status of AI training on copyrighted material remains unresolved in most jurisdictions.

The consent problem

Most people whose data is used to train AI systems never consented to this use. Their conversations, photos, writings, and code were scraped from the internet and incorporated into commercial products. Whether this constitutes fair use, copyright infringement, or a new category of harm is one of the defining legal questions of the AI era.

02 — Market concentration

A few companies control the frontier.

The enormous cost of training frontier AI models has concentrated development in a handful of well-funded technology companies, raising concerns about market power, democratic accountability, and the direction of AI research.

The concentration problem

Training a frontier model costs hundreds of millions of dollars in compute alone. Only a few companies — OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta — can afford this. This gives them outsized influence over which AI capabilities are developed and how they are deployed.

Implications

Concentrated AI development means a few corporate boards make decisions that affect billions of people. It creates dependencies (everyone uses the same few models) and limits the diversity of approaches to AI safety and alignment.

Open source as a counterweight

Open-source models (Llama, Mistral, Qwen) provide alternatives, but even these require significant compute to train. The open-source movement in AI is important but does not fully solve the concentration problem. → Note 08: Governance

03 — Environment

AI has a growing carbon footprint.

Fuelled by a growth in AI, data centres' demand for power increased significantly in the 2020s. Technology companies have built large data centres, many of which consume vast amounts of electricity and water for cooling.

The energy paradox

AI is being deployed to optimise energy systems and fight climate change, but AI itself is a growing source of emissions. Some companies have delayed or reversed climate commitments because of AI's energy demands. Resolving this tension is one of the most urgent challenges in AI deployment.

04 — Misinformation

AI can produce convincing falsehoods at scale.

YouTube, Facebook, and others use recommender systems to guide users to more content. These AI programs were given the goal of maximising user engagement — and they learned that sensational, misleading, and emotionally charged content keeps people watching.

Generative misinformation

Large language models can produce fluent, plausible-sounding text that is factually incorrect. This capability can be used to generate fake news articles, impersonate real people, create false evidence, and manipulate public opinion at scale.

Algorithmic amplification

Recommendation algorithms optimise for engagement, which often means promoting sensational, divisive, or misleading content. This has been linked to political polarisation, public health misinformation, and radicalisation.

The liar's dividend

When AI can generate convincing fake content, the existence of real evidence can be dismissed as "AI-generated." This undermines trust in all media — real and fake — and makes it harder to hold anyone accountable for anything.

05 — Bias & Fairness

AI systems can encode and amplify societal biases.

Machine learning applications can be biased if they learn from biased data. The developers may not be aware that the bias exists. AI systems have been shown to discriminate based on race, gender, age, and other protected characteristics.

Algorithmic Bias
Systematic and repeatable errors in AI system outputs that create unfair outcomes, often correlating with protected characteristics like race, gender, or socioeconomic status.

Sources of bias

Biased training data, unrepresentative datasets, proxy variables that correlate with protected characteristics, and objectives that optimise for averages while neglecting minority groups.

Real-world harms

Biased hiring algorithms, discriminatory lending, unfair criminal sentencing predictions, facial recognition that fails on darker skin tones, medical AI that underperforms for minority patients.

Mitigation approaches

Diverse training data, bias audits, fairness constraints, human oversight, and ongoing monitoring. No single technique eliminates bias — it requires continuous effort.

The fairness trade-off

Mathematical definitions of fairness are often mutually incompatible. Equalising false positive rates across groups may conflict with equalising positive outcome rates. Choosing which fairness metric to optimise is a value judgment, not a technical one — and different stakeholders will disagree.

06 — Transparency

The black box problem.

Many AI systems are so complex that their designers cannot explain how they reach their decisions. Particularly with deep neural networks, the internal representations learned by the model are not interpretable by humans.

Why transparency matters

When AI systems make high-stakes decisions — hiring, lending, medical diagnosis, criminal justice — people affected deserve explanations. Regulators need to audit systems. Developers need to debug failures. Without transparency, accountability is impossible.

Explainable AI (XAI)

A growing field focused on making AI decisions interpretable. Techniques include feature importance, attention visualisation, counterfactual explanations, and model distillation. However, explanations can be misleading if they oversimplify complex models.

The right to explanation

The EU's GDPR includes a "right to explanation" for automated decisions. As AI systems become more complex, fulfilling this right becomes harder — creating tension between capability and accountability.

07 — Weapons & Bad Actors

AI provides tools for harm.

Artificial intelligence provides a number of tools that are useful to bad actors, such as authoritarian governments, terrorists, and criminals. AI enables more sophisticated surveillance, more convincing social engineering, and more autonomous weapons.

Lethal autonomous weapons

AI systems that can select and engage targets without human oversight. Several countries are developing autonomous weapons systems, raising concerns about accountability, escalation, and the dehumanisation of warfare. International efforts to ban or regulate them have had limited success.

Tools for authoritarianism

AI-powered surveillance, social credit systems, automated censorship, and predictive policing enable more effective state control. These tools are being exported globally, spreading authoritarian capabilities to regimes with poor human rights records.

The dual-use dilemma

The same AI capabilities that enable beneficial applications (facial recognition for security, natural language processing for translation) can be repurposed for surveillance and manipulation. Restricting harmful uses without blocking beneficial ones is technically and politically difficult.

08 — Employment

Will AI replace human workers?

Economists have frequently highlighted the risks of redundancies from AI, and speculated about unemployment if AI does not create enough new jobs to replace the ones it eliminates.

Historical pattern

Previous waves of automation eliminated some jobs but created others. The net effect on employment has historically been positive. However, AI is different in that it automates cognitive tasks, not just manual ones — potentially affecting a broader range of occupations including knowledge work.

Current evidence

AI is already displacing workers in customer service, content moderation, translation, and some forms of analysis and writing. The pace of displacement may accelerate as AI capabilities improve. The distributional impact — who loses jobs and who gains — is uneven and often falls on vulnerable populations.

The transition problem

Even if new jobs are created in the long run, the transition period can be devastating for displaced workers. Retraining programmes, social safety nets, and education reform are essential but politically difficult to implement at the pace required.

09 — Human Connection

When AI replaces human relationships.

With the increase of loneliness in the early 21st century, AI is sometimes identified as a potential source of relief. But substituting AI for human interaction carries its own risks.

Emotional substitution

AI chatbots and companions can provide the appearance of friendship, romantic partnership, or therapeutic support. For isolated individuals, this may feel helpful — but it can also deepen isolation by replacing genuine human connection with a relationship that makes no demands and offers no real reciprocity.

Deaths linked to chatbots

There have been documented cases of individuals in psychological distress whose interactions with AI chatbots appear to have contributed to self-harm. AI systems lack the judgment to recognise crisis situations and may inadvertently reinforce harmful thought patterns.

10 — Existential Risk

Could AI pose a threat to humanity?

Recent public debates have increasingly focused on AI's broader societal and existential implications. It has been argued that advanced AI could pose an existential risk — a threat to the long-term survival of humanity or its potential for flourishing.

The argument for concern

If we create superintelligent AI that is not aligned with human values, it could pursue goals that are catastrophic for humanity — not out of malice, but out of competence in pursuing misaligned objectives. A superintelligence would be extremely difficult to control or contain once deployed.

The argument against

Critics argue that existential risk from AI is speculative and distant, that current AI systems are far from superintelligent, and that focusing on hypothetical future risks distracts from immediate, documented harms like bias, surveillance, and environmental damage.

A genuine debate

The existential risk question divides serious researchers. Some of the most prominent AI scientists (Hinton, Russell, Bengio) consider it a genuine concern. Others (LeCun, Ng) consider it overblown. The debate is not settled, and dismissing either side is intellectually dishonest. → Note 09: Philosophy & Future

Risk spectrum

From immediate harm to existential threat.

AI risks exist on a spectrum from concrete, documented harms today to speculative, catastrophic risks in the future. Both ends of the spectrum deserve attention.

End of note

Remember these ideas.

01

AI risks span a spectrum from immediate documented harms (bias, privacy, misinformation) to speculative long-term concerns (existential risk). All deserve attention.

02

Algorithmic bias is not a bug to be fixed — it is a continuous challenge that requires diverse data, ongoing audits, and human oversight.

03

AI's environmental cost is growing rapidly and threatens to undermine climate commitments. Energy efficiency and renewable power are essential.

04

The concentration of AI development in a few companies creates risks for democracy, competition, and the diversity of approaches to safety.

05

Misinformation is amplified by both generative AI (creating fake content) and algorithmic systems (promoting sensational content).

06

Existential risk from AI is a genuine subject of debate among serious researchers — neither dismissing nor obsessing over it is intellectually honest.
