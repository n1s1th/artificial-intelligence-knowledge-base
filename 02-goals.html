The Goals of AI Research

The eight traditional capabilities that define what artificial intelligence tries to achieve — from reasoning and planning to perception and social intelligence.

The general problem of intelligence has been broken into subproblems.

The general problem of simulating (or creating) intelligence has been broken down into subproblems. These consist of specific traits or capabilities that researchers expect an intelligent system to display. The traits described here have received the most attention and cover the scope of AI research.

Goal 01

Reasoning and problem-solving.

Early researchers developed algorithms that imitated step-by-step reasoning that humans use when solving puzzles or making logical deductions. By the late 1980s and 1990s, methods were developed for dealing with uncertain or incomplete information, employing concepts from probability and economics.

Reasoning
The ability to draw logical conclusions from available information, using deduction, induction, or abduction.
The combinatorial explosion

Many of these algorithms were insufficient for solving large reasoning problems because they experienced a "combinatorial explosion" — they become exponentially slower as the size of the problem grows. This limitation drove research into more efficient search methods and heuristic approaches.

Symbolic reasoning

Early AI used explicit rules and logic to reason about the world. Programs could prove mathematical theorems, solve algebra problems, and play games like chess by exploring possible moves.

Strengths: Transparent, explainable, precise

Limits: Brittle, requires explicit knowledge encoding, computationally expensive

Probabilistic reasoning

Modern approaches use probability theory to handle uncertainty. Bayesian networks, Markov models, and other probabilistic frameworks allow systems to reason when information is incomplete or noisy.

Strengths: Handles uncertainty, learns from data, robust

Limits: Less transparent, requires training data

Goal 02

Knowledge representation.

An ontology represents knowledge as a set of concepts within a domain and the relationships between those concepts. AI programs use knowledge to answer questions intelligently and make deductions about the real world.

Knowledge Representation
Methods for encoding facts, concepts, and relationships in a form that AI systems can use for reasoning and decision-making.

Ontologies

Formal representations of concepts and their relationships within a domain. They define what exists and how things relate to each other.

Knowledge graphs

Network structures that store entities and their relationships. Google's Knowledge Graph powers search results with factual information.

Commonsense knowledge

Basic facts about the physical world that humans take for granted — objects fall when dropped, water is wet, people have goals. Encoding this remains challenging.

The knowledge bottleneck

Knowledge-based systems require extensive manual encoding of facts and rules. This made expert systems expensive to build and maintain. Modern machine learning approaches learn representations from data, partially addressing this bottleneck but raising questions about transparency and reliability.

Goal 03

Planning and decision-making.

An "agent" is any entity (artificial or not) that perceives and takes actions in the world. A rational agent has goals or preferences and takes actions to make them happen. In automated planning, the agent has a specific goal. In automated decision-making, the agent has preferences over outcomes and assigns utilities to them.

Agent
Any entity that perceives its environment and takes actions to achieve goals or maximize expected utility.

Automated planning

Finding a sequence of actions that will achieve a goal. Planning algorithms search through possible action sequences, considering preconditions, effects, and constraints.

Applications: Robot navigation, logistics, manufacturing scheduling, space missions

Decision-making under uncertainty

When outcomes are uncertain, agents must make decisions that maximize expected utility. This requires probabilistic models and decision theory.

Applications: Medical diagnosis, financial trading, autonomous vehicles, game playing

Figure 02 · The agent perception-action cycle
Rational agents

A rational agent is one that acts to maximize its expected utility given its beliefs about the world. This framework, drawn from economics and decision theory, provides a mathematical foundation for understanding intelligent behavior.

Goal 04

Learning.

Machine learning is the study of programs that can improve their performance on a given task automatically. It has been a part of AI from the beginning.

Machine Learning
A subset of AI where systems learn patterns or mappings from data, improving their performance on tasks without being explicitly programmed for every case.

Supervised learning

Learning from labeled examples — the training data includes both inputs and desired outputs. The system learns to predict outputs for new inputs.

Examples: Image classification, spam detection, medical diagnosis

Unsupervised learning

Finding patterns in data without labeled outputs. The system discovers structure, clusters, or representations.

Examples: Customer segmentation, anomaly detection, dimensionality reduction

Reinforcement learning

Learning through interaction with an environment. The agent takes actions, receives rewards or penalties, and learns policies that maximize cumulative reward.

Examples: Game playing, robotics, autonomous driving

Why learning matters

Learning addresses the knowledge bottleneck by allowing systems to acquire capabilities from data rather than manual programming. Modern AI's success is largely due to advances in machine learning, particularly deep learning. → Note 04: Machine Learning & Neural Networks

Goal 05

Natural language processing.

Natural language processing (NLP) allows programs to read, write, and communicate in human languages. Specific problems include speech recognition, speech synthesis, machine translation, information extraction, and question answering.

NLP
The field concerned with enabling computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language in useful ways.
NLP TaskDescriptionCurrent Status
Speech recognitionConverting spoken language to textHighly accurate in quiet conditions
Speech synthesisGenerating natural-sounding speech from textVery natural, nearly indistinguishable from humans
Machine translationTranslating text between languagesGood for common languages, struggles with nuance
Sentiment analysisDetermining emotional tone in textEffective for obvious sentiment, subtle cases harder
Question answeringAnswering questions based on provided or retrieved informationImproved dramatically with LLMs, but can hallucinate
Text generationProducing coherent, relevant textFluent but not always factual or logical
The ambiguity problem

Human language is inherently ambiguous. Words have multiple meanings, sentences can be parsed in different ways, and meaning depends on context, tone, and shared knowledge. Modern large language models handle ambiguity through statistical patterns learned from massive text corpora, but they can still fail on subtle cases or produce plausible-sounding but incorrect outputs.

Goal 06

Perception.

Machine perception is the ability to use input from sensors (cameras, microphones, wireless signals, sonar, lidar, and tactile sensors) to deduce aspects of the world.

Machine Perception
The ability of AI systems to interpret sensory data and extract meaningful information about the environment.

Computer vision

Recognizing objects, faces, scenes, and activities in images and video. Deep learning has achieved superhuman performance on many visual recognition tasks.

Speech processing

Recognizing spoken words, understanding prosody and emotion, separating speakers in noisy environments.

Multimodal perception

Combining multiple sensory inputs (vision, audio, touch) to build a richer understanding of the environment.

From pixels to meaning

Perception involves multiple levels of processing: low-level (edges, textures), mid-level (shapes, parts), and high-level (objects, scenes, actions). Deep neural networks learn these representations automatically, but understanding why they make specific decisions remains challenging.

Goal 07

Social intelligence.

Kismet, a robot head made in the 1990s, is a machine that can recognize and simulate emotions. Affective computing is a field that comprises systems that recognize, interpret, process, or simulate human feelings.

Social Intelligence
The ability to recognize, understand, and respond appropriately to human emotions, social cues, and interpersonal dynamics.

Emotion recognition

Detecting emotions from facial expressions, voice tone, body language, and text. Used in customer service, mental health monitoring, and human-robot interaction.

Challenges: Cultural variation, individual differences, context dependence

Social simulation

Generating appropriate emotional responses and social behavior. Important for chatbots, virtual assistants, and social robots that interact with humans.

Challenges: Avoiding uncanny valley, maintaining consistency, ethical concerns about deception

Ethical considerations

Systems that simulate emotions and social cues raise important ethical questions. Can they manipulate users? Should they disclose they are not human? → Note 07: Ethics, Risks & Harms examines these concerns in detail.

Goal 08

General intelligence.

Some companies, such as OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Meta, aim to create artificial general intelligence (AGI) — AI that can complete nearly any cognitive task at least as well as a human.

Artificial General Intelligence
Hypothetical AI systems with broad cognitive capabilities across many domains, able to learn and apply knowledge flexibly like humans.

Narrow AI (current)

Systems that excel at specific tasks but cannot generalize broadly. Examples: chess engines, image classifiers, language models. These systems are highly capable within their domain but lack the flexibility of human intelligence.

General AI (future goal)

Systems that can learn any task a human can learn, transfer knowledge between domains, reason abstractly, and adapt to novel situations. This remains an open research challenge with no clear timeline.

The AGI debate

Experts disagree on whether AGI is achievable, when it might occur, and how to define it precisely. Some argue current foundation models are steps toward AGI; others believe fundamentally new approaches are needed. → Note 09: Philosophy & Future explores these questions in depth.

Connections

How the goals relate to each other.

Interdependencies

Many goals depend on others. Planning requires reasoning about actions and their consequences. Learning requires representing knowledge. Natural language processing requires understanding meaning and context. Perception requires learning to recognize patterns.

Progress varies by goal

Some goals have seen remarkable progress (perception, narrow learning tasks), while others remain challenging (general intelligence, commonsense reasoning, robust planning in complex environments). Progress is not uniform across all goals.

End of note

Remember these ideas.

01

AI research pursues eight traditional goals: reasoning, knowledge representation, planning, learning, NLP, perception, social intelligence, and general intelligence.

02

Reasoning and planning enable agents to make decisions and achieve goals, but face computational challenges like combinatorial explosion.

03

Learning from data (machine learning) has become the dominant paradigm, addressing the knowledge bottleneck of earlier symbolic approaches.

04

Perception and NLP have achieved remarkable progress with deep learning, but challenges remain in handling ambiguity and edge cases.

05

General intelligence (AGI) remains an open challenge — current AI systems are narrow specialists, not general problem-solvers.
