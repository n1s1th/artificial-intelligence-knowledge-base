Foundations of Artificial Intelligence

What AI is, how the field evolved through cycles of optimism and disappointment, and why the current era represents a fundamental shift in computational intelligence.

FOUNDATIONREADING · 15–20 MINPREREQUISITE · NONEKNOWLEDGE BASE · 01 / 09

A field defined by capability, not by any single technology.

Artificial intelligence is the capability of computational systems to perform tasks typically associated with human intelligence: learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and decision-making. It is a field of research in engineering, mathematics, and computer science that develops methods and software enabling machines to perceive their environment and take actions that maximize their chances of achieving defined goals.

01 — Definition

AI is a capability, not a product.

Artificial Intelligence
The capability of computational systems to perform tasks typically associated with human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and decision-making.

High-profile applications of AI include advanced web search engines, chatbots, virtual assistants, autonomous vehicles, play and analysis in strategy games (e.g., chess and Go), and content generation (images, audio, and videos). The traditional goals of AI research include learning, reasoning, knowledge representation, planning, natural language processing, and perception, as well as support for robotics.

The interdisciplinary nature

AI draws upon psychology, linguistics, philosophy, neuroscience, and other fields. It is not purely a computer science discipline — it sits at the intersection of multiple fields, each contributing insights about intelligence, cognition, and computation.

Engineering

Designing and building systems that exhibit intelligent behavior in practice.

Mathematics

Providing formal frameworks for reasoning, optimization, probability, and learning theory.

Computer Science

Developing algorithms, data structures, and computational models for intelligent systems.

02 — Scope

What AI researchers work on.

AI research addresses a wide range of capabilities, from narrow tasks (playing chess, recognizing faces) to the ambitious goal of artificial general intelligence (AGI) — systems that can complete nearly any cognitive task at least as well as a human.

Narrow vs. general AI

Most current AI systems are narrow — highly capable within specific domains but unable to generalize broadly. Artificial general intelligence (AGI) remains a long-term goal pursued by some companies (OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta), but has not been achieved. → Note 09: Philosophy & Future

03 — History

Sixty years of cycles.

Artificial intelligence was founded as an academic discipline in 1956. The field went through multiple cycles of optimism throughout its history, followed by periods of disappointment and loss of funding known as AI winters.

The founding (1956)

The Dartmouth workshop brought together researchers who believed that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." This optimism fueled early breakthroughs and attracted significant funding.

Early successes (1950s–60s)

Researchers developed programs that could solve algebra word problems, prove theorems, and learn to speak English. By the mid-1960s, the U.S. Department of Defense was heavily funding AI research, and optimism ran high.

First AI winter (1974–80)

Expectations exceeded reality. Computers lacked sufficient memory and processing power for complex tasks. Governments cut funding after disappointing results. The field entered its first period of reduced interest and investment.

Expert systems boom (1980s)

Expert systems — rule-based programs that mimicked human experts — created a commercial market. Funding returned, and the AI industry grew rapidly. Japan's Fifth Generation Computer project spurred competition.

Second AI winter (1987–93)

Expert systems proved expensive to maintain and brittle in practice. The market collapsed, and funding dried up again. The term "AI winter" was coined during this period.

Quiet progress (1990s–2000s)

Researchers focused on narrower, more achievable goals. Statistical methods, machine learning, and neural networks made steady progress. Deep Blue defeated chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997.

The pattern

AI history shows a recurring pattern: bold promises → initial progress → overpromising → disappointment → funding cuts → recovery. Understanding this pattern is crucial for evaluating current claims about AI capabilities and timelines.

05 — AI Winters

When progress stalled and funding vanished.

AI winters are periods of reduced interest, funding, and progress in artificial intelligence research. They occur when expectations exceed technological capabilities and promised breakthroughs fail to materialize.

First winter (1974–1980)

Causes: Insufficient computing power, combinatorial explosion problems, failure of machine translation, Lighthill report criticizing AI progress.

Effects: DARPA cut funding, research slowed, skepticism grew. AI became associated with overpromising.

Second winter (1987–1993)

Causes: Expert systems proved expensive and brittle, desktop computers overtook specialized Lisp machines, Fifth Generation Computer project disappointed.

Effects: AI market collapsed from $1 billion to $100 million, researchers avoided the term "AI" to escape stigma.

Lessons from the winters

AI winters teach that technological progress is non-linear. Breakthroughs require not just clever algorithms but sufficient hardware, data, and infrastructure. Overpromising damages the entire field and delays progress.

06 — Modern Era

The breakthroughs that changed everything.

Funding and interest increased substantially after 2012, when graphics processing units (GPUs) started being used to accelerate neural networks, and deep learning outperformed previous AI techniques. This growth accelerated further after 2017 with the transformer architecture. In the 2020s, an AI boom coincided with advances in generative AI, which became widespread and allowed for the creation and modification of media.

GPU Revolution
Graphics processing units, originally designed for rendering video games, proved highly effective for training neural networks due to their parallel processing capabilities. This made deep learning practical for the first time.
Deep Learning
Machine learning using multi-layer neural networks that can learn increasingly abstract representations of data. Deep learning outperformed previous techniques on tasks like image recognition, speech processing, and natural language understanding.
Transformer Architecture
A neural network architecture introduced in 2017 that uses attention mechanisms to process sequential data more efficiently than previous approaches. Transformers enabled large language models like GPT, BERT, and their successors.

2012: AlexNet

A deep convolutional neural network won the ImageNet competition by a large margin, demonstrating that deep learning could achieve superhuman performance on visual recognition tasks.

2017: Attention

The paper "Attention Is All You Need" introduced transformers, replacing recurrent networks with a more efficient attention mechanism that could be parallelized across GPUs.

2022: ChatGPT

OpenAI's ChatGPT demonstrated that large language models could engage in fluent conversation, write code, and assist with diverse tasks. Generative AI became mainstream.

Why this boom is different

Previous AI cycles focused on narrow capabilities (chess, expert systems). The current boom is built on foundation models — large neural networks trained on massive datasets that can be adapted to many tasks. 01

AI is the capability of computational systems to perform tasks associated with human intelligence — not a single technology but a broad field.

02

AI research has pursued eight traditional goals: reasoning, knowledge representation, planning, learning, NLP, perception, social intelligence, and general intelligence.

03

The field has experienced cycles of optimism and disappointment, with two major AI winters (1974–80, 1987–93) caused by overpromising and underdelivering.

04

Three breakthroughs enabled modern AI: GPUs for parallel computation (2012), deep learning architectures, and transformers (2017).

05

The current AI boom differs from previous cycles because foundation models exhibit broad generalization and emergent capabilities, not just narrow task performance.
