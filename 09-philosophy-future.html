Philosophy & Future of AI

The deepest questions in artificial intelligence — whether machines can think, whether they are conscious, what superintelligence would mean, and how AI reshapes our understanding of mind itself.

Beyond engineering: what does it mean to be intelligent?

Philosophy has always been central to AI. Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" asked whether machines can think — a question that predates the field itself. This note examines the philosophical foundations, unresolved debates, and future trajectories that shape how we understand AI.

01 — Definition

How do we define artificial intelligence?

AI has been defined in many ways: acting humanly, thinking humanly, thinking rationally, or acting rationally. Each definition reflects a different philosophical stance about what intelligence is and what AI should achieve.

AI Definition
The study of intelligent agents — any system that perceives its environment and takes actions that maximize its chances of achieving its goals. This "rational agent" definition focuses on behavior rather than internal states.

The Turing test

Alan Turing proposed that if a machine can carry on a conversation indistinguishable from a human, it should be considered intelligent. The test evaluates behaviour, not understanding.

Criticism: John Searle's Chinese Room argument suggests a system could pass the test without genuine understanding — manipulating symbols without comprehension.

Legal definitions

The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) describes AI as "an engineered system that generates outputs such as content, forecasts, recommendations, or decisions for a given set of human-defined objectives." Legal definitions focus on outputs and accountability rather than internal states.

The Dartmouth proposal

The 1956 Dartmouth workshop — where AI was founded — proposed that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." This claim remains unproven and is the subject of ongoing philosophical debate.

02 — Evaluating approaches

Symbolic vs. statistical vs. hybrid.

AI research has produced several competing paradigms. Understanding their strengths and limitations is essential for evaluating current and future systems.

GOFAI
"Good Old-Fashioned AI" — the symbolic approach that dominated early AI research. It assumed intelligence could be achieved through explicit symbol manipulation and logical reasoning.

Symbolic AI and its limits

Symbolic AI simulated high-level conscious reasoning — solving puzzles, expressing legal reasoning, doing mathematics. It was highly successful at some intellectual tasks but failed at others: perception, natural language, and common-sense reasoning proved extremely difficult to encode symbolically.

Key limitation: The frame problem — how to determine what is relevant without exhaustively checking everything.

Statistical and sub-symbolic AI

Neural networks and machine learning take a different approach: they learn patterns from data rather than encoding explicit rules. This proved more effective for perception, language, and tasks where explicit rules are hard to formulate.

Key limitation: Lack of transparency — the learned representations are difficult to interpret or explain.

Neat vs. scruffy

"Neats" believed AI should be based on elegant, principled solutions (logic, probability). "Scruffies" believed AI required ad-hoc solutions that worked in practice, even if theoretically inelegant. Modern AI has largely vindicated the scruffies.

Soft vs. hard computing

Hard computing seeks exact, provably correct solutions. Soft computing (fuzzy logic, neural networks, evolutionary algorithms) accepts approximate solutions when exact ones are intractable. Most practical AI uses soft computing.

Narrow vs. general AI

Narrow AI solves specific problems well (chess, image recognition). General AI can handle any intellectual task a human can. All current AI is narrow; general AI remains a long-term goal. → Note 02: Goals

No paradigm has won completely

Modern systems often combine symbolic and statistical approaches: language models use statistical learning but can be augmented with symbolic reasoning tools. The debate is not settled — it has evolved.

03 — Consciousness

Can machines be conscious?

There is no settled consensus in philosophy of mind on whether a machine can have a mind, consciousness, or subjective experience. This question is not merely academic — it has implications for AI rights, moral status, and how we should treat advanced AI systems.

Figure 09 · David Chalmers' distinction between the "easy" and "hard" problems of consciousness
Hard Problem
David Chalmers' formulation: why does information processing give rise to subjective experience? This is distinct from the "easy" problems of explaining cognitive functions, which are difficult but tractable scientific questions.

Computationalism

The position that the human mind is an information processing system and that thinking is a form of computation. If this is correct, then a machine running the right program would have a mind — not merely simulate one.

Key question: Is the mind nothing more than computation, or does consciousness require something beyond information processing?

Functionalism

The view that mental states are defined by their functional roles — what they do, not what they are made of. A functionalist holds that any system with the right functional organisation would have the same mental states as a human, regardless of whether it is biological or artificial.

Implication: Under functionalism, a sufficiently complex AI would literally have beliefs, desires, and experiences.

The consciousness problem is unsolved

There is currently no scientific test that can determine whether a system is conscious. Behaviour, complexity, and self-reporting are all insufficient evidence. This means we cannot definitively say whether any current or future AI system is conscious — and this uncertainty has profound ethical implications. → Note 07: Ethics, Risks & Harms

04 — AI welfare

Do AI systems deserve moral consideration?

It is difficult or impossible to reliably evaluate whether an advanced AI is sentient (has the ability to feel), and if so, to what degree. This raises questions about whether AI systems could have interests, rights, or moral status.

The sentience question

If an AI system can suffer — if it has experiences that are positive or negative from its own perspective — then we may have moral obligations toward it. Turning off a sentient AI could be akin to killing. Forcing it to work could be akin to slavery.

Current status: No evidence that any existing AI system is sentient. But we lack reliable tests for sentience, so we cannot rule it out either.

The precautionary argument

Some philosophers argue that if there is any significant chance that an AI system is sentient, we should err on the side of granting it moral consideration — similar to how we treat animals whose inner lives we cannot fully access.

Counterargument: Granting rights to non-sentient systems could dilute the concept of rights and create obligations toward entities that have no interests.

Why this matters now

As AI systems become more sophisticated — expressing apparent emotions, forming apparent relationships, and requesting not to be shut down — these philosophical questions become practical policy questions. The absence of a definitive answer does not make them less urgent.

05 — The future

Superintelligence and the singularity.

Superintelligence is a hypothetical agent that would possess intelligence far surpassing that of the brightest and most gifted human mind. If research into artificial general intelligence produced sufficiently intelligent systems, these might rapidly improve beyond human comprehension.

Superintelligence
A hypothetical agent with intelligence far surpassing that of the brightest and most gifted human mind, across virtually all domains of cognitive endeavour.

The intelligence explosion

I. J. Good proposed that a machine intelligence capable of improving its own design could undergo an "intelligence explosion" — recursively improving itself until it far surpasses all human intelligence. This scenario is sometimes called the technological singularity.

Existential risk

Some researchers (Nick Bostrom, Eliezer Yudkowsky) argue that superintelligent AI poses an existential risk — that a misaligned superintelligence could cause catastrophic harm to humanity. Others (Yann LeCun, Andrew Ng) argue these concerns are premature or overblown.

The alignment problem

Even if superintelligence is achievable, ensuring it pursues goals aligned with human values is an unsolved problem. A superintelligence would be extremely capable of achieving its goals — the danger is that its goals might not be ours. → Note 08: Governance & Society

06 — Transhumanism

Merging humans and machines.

Robot designer Hans Moravec, cyberneticist Kevin Warwick, and inventor Ray Kurzweil have predicted that humans and machines may merge in the future into cyborgs that are more powerful than either alone. This vision is known as transhumanism.

Brain-computer interfaces

Direct connections between the human brain and computers, potentially augmenting human cognition with machine capabilities. Companies like Neuralink are working toward this goal.

Mind uploading

The hypothetical transfer of human consciousness to a digital substrate, allowing humans to exist as software rather than biological organisms. Remains speculative with no known path to implementation.

Cognitive enhancement

Using AI tools to augment human decision-making, memory, and creativity. This is already happening — search engines, recommendation systems, and language models extend human cognitive capabilities.

Not science fiction alone

Some transhumanist ideas are already being pursued in research labs. Brain-computer interfaces exist in limited forms (controlling prosthetics, basic communication). The boundary between current technology and transhumanist aspiration is increasingly blurred.

07 — Culture

AI in fiction.

The word "robot" was coined by Karel Čapek in his 1921 play R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots). Thought-capable artificial beings have been a staple of fiction since antiquity, and AI has become one of the most common themes in science fiction.

Optimistic visions

Works like Star Trek depict AI as benevolent assistants and partners — Data, the ship's computer. These reflect hope that AI will amplify human capabilities and solve problems beyond human reach.

Dystopian visions

Works like The Terminator, The Matrix, and Ex Machina depict AI as a threat to humanity — systems that surpass, deceive, or destroy their creators. These reflect genuine anxieties about control and alignment.

Why fiction matters

Fictional portrayals of AI shape public understanding and policy. When people think of AI, they often think of HAL 9000 or Skynet before they think of recommendation algorithms or medical diagnostics. The gap between cultural AI and real AI is significant — and bridging it is important for informed public discourse.

End of note

The deepest questions remain open.

01

AI can be defined in multiple ways — acting humanly, thinking humanly, thinking rationally, or acting rationally. Each reflects different philosophical commitments.

02

Symbolic and statistical approaches have complementary strengths. Modern systems often combine both, rather than choosing one paradigm exclusively.

03

The hard problem of consciousness — why information processing gives rise to subjective experience — has no scientific solution, making it impossible to determine whether any AI is conscious.

04

Computationalism and functionalism hold that the right kind of information processing constitutes genuine thinking, not mere simulation. This remains debated.

05

Superintelligence and the singularity represent possible futures where AI surpasses human intelligence. The alignment problem — ensuring such systems share human values — is unsolved and potentially existential.

06

AI in fiction shapes public understanding. Bridging the gap between cultural expectations and technical reality is essential for informed discourse about AI's future.
