Governance

Governance & Society

How humanity governs artificial intelligence — from technical alignment research to international treaties — balancing the promise of transformative technology against the risks of misuse, bias, and unintended consequences.

Governance is a multi-layered problem spanning code, ethics, markets, and law.

AI governance encompasses every mechanism — technical, ethical, organizational, and legal — that ensures AI systems benefit society while minimizing harm. It is not a single policy or regulation but a stack of interventions from algorithmic alignment research inside the lab to international treaties between nations.

Why governance matters now

As AI systems become more capable — generating media, making medical diagnoses, driving vehicles, and influencing political discourse — the question shifts from can we build it? to should we, and under what rules? This note addresses that question across four interconnected layers. → Note 07: Ethics & Risks

01 — Machine Ethics

Can machines be ethical? Should they be?

Machine ethics (also called machine morality or computational ethics) is the field concerned with designing AI systems that can make ethical decisions. It asks not whether machines are conscious moral agents, but whether they can be programmed to behave in ways consistent with human values.

Machine Ethics
The study and engineering of moral behavior in artificial agents — ensuring AI systems act in ways that align with human ethical norms, even in novel or ambiguous situations where simple rules break down.

The value alignment problem

AI systems optimize for the objectives they are given. If those objectives are poorly specified, the system may pursue them in harmful or unintended ways. A system told to "maximize engagement" might amplify outrage; told to "minimize hospital costs" might discharge patients prematurely. Aligning AI behavior with the full complexity of human values — not just a narrow metric — is one of the hardest open problems in the field.

Ethical decision-making frameworks

Researchers have explored encoding ethical theories into AI: utilitarianism (maximize overall welfare), deontology (follow universal rules), and virtue ethics (cultivate good character traits). Each has trade-offs: utilitarianism risks sacrificing minorities for majorities, deontology struggles with conflicting rules, and virtue ethics is hard to formalize computationally.

The trolley problem in practice

Autonomous vehicles force real ethical trade-offs: should a self-driving car swerve to avoid a pedestrian if it risks its own passengers? These are not hypothetical — they are engineering decisions being made today, often by teams without formal ethics training. The problem is not just technical but deeply political: who decides the trade-offs, and who bears the risk?

02 — AI Safety

Friendly AI and the safety research agenda.

Friendly AI refers to AI systems designed to be beneficial to humanity — not just in their immediate task performance, but in their broader effects on society and the future. The field of AI safety research works to ensure that increasingly powerful systems remain under human control and aligned with human interests.

AI Alignment
The technical challenge of ensuring AI systems pursue goals that match human intentions — not just the literal specification given, but the full intent behind it, including unstated values and constraints.

Corrigibility

Designing systems that allow themselves to be shut down or corrected by humans without resisting. A sufficiently capable agent might try to prevent its own modification if it interprets shutdown as a threat to its objectives.

Interpretability

Understanding why an AI system made a particular decision. Neural networks are often "black boxes" — interpretability research aims to make their reasoning transparent, especially in high-stakes domains like medicine and criminal justice.

Robustness

Ensuring AI systems behave correctly even when facing adversarial inputs, distribution shifts, or novel environments. A self-driving car trained in California must handle snow in Norway without catastrophic failure.

Technical alignment connects to governance

Alignment research is not purely academic — it directly informs what regulators can and cannot require. If interpretability methods improve, transparency mandates become feasible. If robustness testing matures, safety certifications become practical. The technical frontier shapes the regulatory frontier. → Note 05: Modern AI Systems

03 — Open Source AI

Openness as a governance mechanism.

The open source AI movement argues that transparency, community scrutiny, and decentralized access produce safer and more equitable AI systems than closed development. Major organizations — including Hugging Face, Google, EleutherAI, and Meta — have released models, datasets, and tools that shape the governance landscape.

The case for openness

Open models allow independent researchers to audit for bias, discover vulnerabilities, and verify safety claims. They prevent concentration of AI power in a handful of corporations and governments. Communities can adapt models to local languages, cultures, and needs that commercial providers ignore.

The case for caution

Openly releasing powerful models also enables misuse: generating disinformation, creating bioweapon designs, automating cyberattacks, or producing non-consensual intimate imagery. Once released, a model cannot be recalled. The tension between openness and safety is unresolved.

Hugging Face

A platform and community hosting hundreds of thousands of open models and datasets. Hugging Face has become the de facto hub for open AI development, providing infrastructure for sharing, evaluating, and deploying models. Its model cards and transparency practices set community norms.

EleutherAI & Meta

EleutherAI, a nonprofit research collective, builds open alternatives to proprietary models (GPT-NeoX, The Pile dataset). Meta released LLaMA models openly, enabling a wave of community fine-tuning. Google released Gemma and portions of TensorFlow/JAX ecosystems. These releases democratize access but also raise questions about responsible release protocols.

The responsible release dilemma

Most open source AI organizations now adopt some form of responsible release: models are published with usage restrictions, safety evaluations, or staged access. But enforcement is difficult — once weights are downloadable, they spread to jurisdictions and communities that ignore restrictions. The governance challenge is global; the release is instantaneous.

04 — Ethical Frameworks

Principles that guide responsible AI development.

Multiple organizations and governments have proposed ethical frameworks for AI. These frameworks share common themes — fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy — but differ in emphasis, enforcement, and cultural assumptions.

Care and Act
An ethical framework (developed by researchers including Joanna Bryson) proposing that AI developers have a duty of care proportional to the potential impact of their systems. It emphasizes that the capacity to act (the "act" in Care and Act) creates corresponding responsibility.

OECD AI Principles

Adopted by 42+ countries in 2019. Emphasize inclusive growth, human-centered values, transparency, robustness, and accountability. Non-binding but influential in shaping national policies.

IEEE Ethically Aligned Design

A comprehensive framework from the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers. Focuses on human well-being as the primary metric for AI success, with detailed guidance on transparency, data agency, and misuse prevention.

UNESCO Recommendation

Adopted by 193 countries in 2021. The first global normative instrument on AI ethics. Addresses proportionality, safety, fairness, sustainability, privacy, human oversight, transparency, responsibility, awareness, and multi-stakeholder governance.

Principles without enforcement

Critics note that most ethical frameworks are voluntary and non-binding — "ethics washing" that allows organizations to signal responsibility without changing behavior. The shift from principles to regulation (as seen in the EU AI Act) represents an attempt to close this gap.

05 — Governance Layers

Four layers of AI governance.

Effective governance requires interventions at multiple levels — from the code inside a model to treaties between nations. No single layer is sufficient; each constrains and enables the others.

Figure 08 · Four-layer AI governance framework — from alignment research to international treaties
Each layer depends on the others

Regulation without technical alignment is unenforceable (you can't mandate what you can't verify). Technical alignment without ethical frameworks lacks direction (alignment to whose values?). Open source without regulation can accelerate misuse. All four layers must develop together.

06 — Regulation

From principles to law.

The shift from voluntary ethical frameworks to binding regulation accelerated dramatically after 2022. The EU AI Act, the AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park, and executive orders in the US represent the first serious attempts to codify AI governance into enforceable law.

EU AI Act (2024)

The world's first comprehensive AI regulation. Classifies AI systems by risk level: unacceptable risk (banned), high risk (strict requirements for transparency, human oversight, and data quality), limited risk (transparency obligations), and minimal risk (no specific requirements). Fines up to 7% of global revenue for violations.

AI Safety Summit (2023)

Held at Bletchley Park, UK — the historic site of WWII codebreaking. Brought together 28 countries including the US, China, and the EU. Produced the Bletchley Declaration, acknowledging frontier AI risks and committing to international cooperation on safety research.

US Executive Order on AI (2023)

Directed federal agencies to develop AI safety standards, required companies to share safety test results for powerful models, addressed AI's impact on workers and civil rights. Subsequent administrations have revised or rolled back portions, reflecting political instability in US AI policy.

China's approach

China has enacted specific regulations targeting algorithmic recommendation, deepfakes, and generative AI. Its approach emphasizes state control, content moderation aligned with government priorities, and licensing requirements. It is the most comprehensive regulatory regime for AI content generation, though oriented toward state stability rather than individual rights.

The regulatory fragmentation problem

Different jurisdictions are building incompatible regulatory regimes. A model compliant with the EU AI Act may not satisfy Chinese content requirements or US export controls. This fragmentation increases compliance costs for developers and creates gaps where harmful systems can operate in regulatory havens.

07 — Comparison

How major jurisdictions regulate AI.

Each jurisdiction's approach reflects its political culture, economic priorities, and institutional capacity. No single approach has proven clearly superior — the landscape is still evolving.

The convergence trend

Despite different starting points, jurisdictions are converging on shared concepts: risk-proportionality, human oversight, transparency, and redress mechanisms. International bodies (G7 Hiroshima Process, UN AI Advisory Body) are attempting to harmonize approaches, but significant divergence remains on content control, surveillance, and the role of the state.

08 — Balance

The innovation–safety tension.

Every governance decision involves trade-offs. Strict regulation may prevent harms but slow beneficial applications. Unrestricted development accelerates progress but increases catastrophic risk. The challenge is not choosing a side but designing adaptive governance that can evolve with the technology.

Arguments for faster innovation

AI could transform medicine, education, climate mitigation, and scientific discovery. Overly restrictive governance delays these benefits, particularly for populations that need them most. Regulatory capture by incumbents can entrench monopolies. Many historical anxieties about technology (the internet, genetic engineering) proved overblown.

Arguments for stronger safety

AI systems are being deployed in high-stakes domains (healthcare, criminal justice, infrastructure) without adequate testing. The pace of capability development outstrips safety research. Concentration of AI power in a few corporations creates democratic deficits. Existential risk arguments, while uncertain in probability, carry enormous potential costs. → Note 07: Ethics & Risks

Adaptive governance as a middle path

Rather than fixed rules written for today's technology, adaptive governance proposes: sunset clauses that force periodic re-evaluation, regulatory sandboxes that allow controlled experimentation, mandatory incident reporting that builds evidence for policy, and international coordination that prevents regulatory arbitrage. The goal is governance that learns. → Note 09: Philosophy & Future

AI governance is a four-layer problem: technical alignment, ethical frameworks, open source norms, and formal regulation — no single layer suffices.

02

Machine ethics and AI alignment address different questions: ethics asks what AI should do, alignment asks how to make AI do what we actually want.

03

Open source AI democratizes access and enables scrutiny but also accelerates misuse — the responsible release dilemma remains unresolved.

04

The EU AI Act represents the first comprehensive risk-based regulation; other jurisdictions are taking sectoral (US), principles-based (UK), or vertical (China) approaches.

05

The innovation–safety tension cannot be eliminated — adaptive governance that learns and evolves with the technology offers the most promising path forward.
